## RESPONSE 2: Scientific research (psychology and science of how a website needs to be)

### 1. Scientific evidence base

A. Deciding to stay, first 50 ms to 10 seconds. Lindgaard et al. (2006, Behaviour and Information Technology): users form a reliable visual impression in 50 ms, correlating at r = 0.97 with impressions after 500 ms. Tuch et al. (2012, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 119 real websites, 17 ms to 1,000 ms): low visual complexity + high prototypicality (familiar SaaS layout: top nav, clear hero, recognizable CTA) judged most appealing within 50 ms; novel layouts trigger instant friction. Liu, White, and Dumais (2010, ACM SIGIR, Weibull survival analysis): "negative aging" in web dwell time, abandonment hazard highest in the first 10 seconds; once a visitor finds relevant value and crosses that threshold, probability of leaving drops sharply. Nielsen (1993): 0.1 s instantaneous, 1.0 s ceiling for uninterrupted flow of thought, 10 s maximum before attention wanders unless progress feedback appears. Nah (2004, Behaviour and Information Technology): tolerable waiting time for simple web retrieval roughly 2 seconds; for slower operations (like a live website audit), a visible progress/feedback indicator extends average tolerance from 13 seconds (no feedback) to 38 seconds (with feedback).

B. The three-click myth vs. what research actually says. Porter (2003, User Interface Engineering, Testing the Three-Click Rule): zero drop-off spike after three clicks, no correlation between fewer clicks and higher satisfaction; users willingly clicked up to 25 times without frustration as long as each click felt like clear progress. Users complain about "too many clicks" only when they fail to find what they want. Information Foraging Theory (Pirolli and Card, 1999, Psychological Review): humans forage for information like organisms forage for food, maximizing value per unit of effort; every link/tab/button must carry unmistakable information scent. Interaction cost (Raluca Budiu, Nielsen Norman Group): combined physical effort (taps, typing, scrolling) and cognitive effort (reading, deciding, remembering). One confusing click costs far more than three effortless, obvious clicks. Hick-Hyman Law (Hick 1952, Hyman 1953): decision time increases logarithmically with the number of simultaneous choices; cramming 15 controls onto one screen backfires.

C. Cognitive load, working memory, choice overload. Sweller (1988, Cognitive Science): Cognitive Load Theory separates intrinsic load (the task) from extraneous load (UI clutter, jargon, visual noise). Cowan (2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences): short-term working memory holds only about 4 independent chunks of new information at once, not Miller's 7+-2. Iyengar and Lepper (2000, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology): extensive option arrays (24-30 items) attract initial curiosity but depress actual conversion vs. limited arrays (6 items). Chernev, Bockenholt, and Goodman (2015, Journal of Consumer Psychology, meta-analysis): choice overload spikes when four moderators are high: decision task difficulty, choice set complexity, preference uncertainty, effort-minimizing goal.

D. Attention, scrolling, reading. Fessenden (2018, Nielsen Norman Group, eye-tracking): users spend 57% of total page viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls. Minimalist hero sections that look "finished" create a false floor ("illusion of completeness") that stops users from scrolling. Pernice (2018, NN/g): web visitors default to F-shaped scanning, even on mobile. Weinreich et al. (2008, ACM Transactions on the Web, 59,573 page views): 17% of views lasted under 4 seconds; half of new pages exited within ~12 seconds. Nielsen (2008) reanalysis: each extra 100 words buys only 4.4 additional seconds; visitors read at most 28% of words on an average 593-word page.

E. Trust signals and perceived credibility. Fogg et al. (2002, Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab, 2,684 participants): 46.1% of credibility comments cited Design Look (layout, typography, consistency), 28.5% Information Design/Structure, 25.1% Information Focus, 15.5% Company Motive, 14.8% Information Usefulness. Prominence-Interpretation Theory: an element boosts trust only if a user both notices it and interprets it positively. Robins and Holmes (2008, Information Processing and Management): identical content in low- vs. high-aesthetic layouts; credibility judgments in an average of 3.42 seconds, same content rated significantly more credible in cleaner design. Fogg's Stanford Web Credibility guidelines: visible contact details/real operator identity, third-party verifiability, recent maintenance signals, restraint with hype, zero typos or broken interactions drive trust.

F. Friction, abandonment, behavior triggers. Fogg Behavior Model B = MAP (Fogg 2009, ACM Persuasive): behavior occurs only when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt converge above the Action Line; Ability is constrained by its weakest link (time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine fit). Removing friction beats manufacturing hype. Baymard Institute (2026, 50-study synthesis): average online abandonment 70.22%. Top kill switches excluding "just browsing": 40% unexpected extra costs/fees, 19% lack of trust with credit card info, 18% forced account creation, 17% overly long/complicated checkout, 17% website errors/crashes, 12% inability to see total cost up-front. Average US checkouts show 14.88 form fields (23.48 elements).

G. How humans decide to pay, price anchoring, staying to buy. Prelec and Loewenstein (1998, Marketing Science): pain of paying; one-time prepayment eliminates monthly mental "red ink" while linking the single payment tightly to clear imputed benefits. Knutson, Rick, Wimmer, Prelec, and Loewenstein (2007, Neuron, fMRI): anticipating product value activates the nucleus accumbens (NAcc), excessive price activates the right insula (pain/disgust circuit) and deactivates the mesial prefrontal cortex (MPFC), predicting purchase or refusal before conscious choice. Kahneman and Tversky (1979, Econometrica, Prospect Theory): losses loom larger than equivalent gains; framing around stopping recurring $696+/yr subscription bleed and preventing lost local leads hits harder than generic "gain" copy. Tversky and Kahneman (1974, Science): anchoring, humans estimate unknown values by anchoring on the first salient reference number and adjusting insufficiently. Ariely, Loewenstein, and Prelec (2003, Quarterly Journal of Economics): "coherent arbitrariness", buyers have no fixed internal price for unfamiliar software until an initial anchor imprints their scale; showing $696/yr before $49 once makes $49 register as an obvious bargain. Huber, Payne, and Puto (1982, Journal of Consumer Research): adding an asymmetrically dominated or range-extending option lifts selection of the target option, provided task complexity stays low. Simonson (1989, Journal of Consumer Research): compromise effect (t = 4.27, p < 0.001), buyers disproportionately pick the middle tier, stronger when the buyer must justify the decision to others (t = 2.19, p < 0.05), exact match for local business owners justifying a website spend to a partner or spouse. Nunes and Dreze (2006, Journal of Consumer Research): endowed progress effect, reframing an 8-step goal as 2-of-10 already completed nearly doubled completion (34% vs. 19%), powered by Zeigarnik (1927) tension over uncompleted tasks. Norton, Mochon, and Ariely (2012, Journal of Consumer Psychology): IKEA effect, small successful co-creation labor raises perceived value and willingness to pay. Letting a visitor run an audit, toggle findings, and preview their own custom branded proposal before paying $49 leverages both effects.
